# The Baha'i Perspective on Service

### Opening Questions

1. Think of someone you've known whom you admire for how much they give to others. Let's honor them: tell us about them, and what it is you admire.

2. When you think of a life that truly mattered, whose comes to mind, and what makes it stand out to you?

3. When you've watched someone act with real goodness (patience, courage, putting others first), where do you think it comes from in them?

### Reading

We were made in the image of God.

It's a claim several of the world's religions share, and the Baha'i teachings hold it too. It doesn't mean God has a face like ours. It means each of us was given the capacity to reflect God's own qualities: love, honesty, courage, compassion, generosity. We are something like mirrors, built to carry a light that doesn't begin in us. The light that shines in such a mirror was never the mirror's own; it is the light of God, borrowed and passed along.

The Writings describe two sides to our nature. One is this higher, spiritual self, the part that can reflect God. The other is a lower, self-centered nature, the ego that thinks first and last of itself. Both are real, and much of a human life is the quiet contest between them.

This is what gives service its meaning. Every time we act with genuine love or patience or generosity, we let that higher nature show, which is to say we let God's own qualities shine out into the world. The good is not, in the end, our private invention. It is God's, passing through us. As the scripture says, *"all good cometh from God and all evil from your own selves."* The one thing that blocks the light is the ego. So the spiritual life is, in large part, the work of getting ourselves out of the way, clearing the channel so that what is best in us, which is really what is best in God, can flow through. And the clearing happens not by waiting until we feel holy enough, but by acting:

> Have faith and confidence, that the Power of the Holy Spirit will flow through you, the right way will appear, the door will open, the right message, the right principle or the right book will be given to you… Act as though it had all been answered. Then act with tireless, ceaseless energy. And, as you act, you yourself will become a magnet which will attract more power to your being, until you become an unobstructed channel for the Divine Power to flow through you.

And this is not some private spiritual hobby. Look honestly at the world's troubles and it's tempting to call them material: broken systems, scarce resources, failing institutions. Shoghi Effendi named the deeper problem:

> Indeed, the chief reason for the evils now rampant in society is a lack of spirituality… The universal crisis affecting mankind is, therefore, essentially spiritual in its causes.

New laws and better organizations help, but without inner change they go hollow. Even religion, he warned, can *"degenerate into a mere organization, and become a dead thing."* What the world is starving for is people whose own inner lives have been transformed and who carry that outward into everything they touch.

This is the highest thing a person can do, and the Faith asks us to keep it in front of us. A short prayer that many Baha'is say every day names the whole purpose of a life in a single line: *"I bear witness that Thou hast created me to know Thee and to worship Thee."* We were made to know God and to worship Him. And worship, in this Faith, is not mainly something that happens in a quiet room with the eyes closed. To worship God is to let His qualities live through what we do. *"Service is prayer,"* Abdu'l-Baha taught, which is how an ordinary action, a kindness, a task done with love, becomes a prayer in its own right:

> Therefore strive that your actions day by day may be beautiful prayers. Turn towards God, and seek always to do that which is right and noble. Enrich the poor, raise the fallen, comfort the sorrowful, bring healing to the sick, reassure the fearful, rescue the oppressed, bring hope to the hopeless, shelter the destitute!

That last line is already pointing somewhere: out the door, toward actual people. Which is the question Section 2 takes up, what carrying this light into the world looks like, day after day.

### Questions for Discussion

1. The reading says the good we do isn't finally ours, it's God's, flowing through us. Does that lift a burden off you, or take something away? How does it actually sit?

2. The reading describes two natures: a higher self that reflects God and a lower self that thinks only of itself. Do you recognize that tug-of-war in your own life? Where do you feel it most sharply?

3. If our daily actions can be "beautiful prayers," does that change what a religious life even is? Could someone who never sets foot in a temple be living a deeply worshipful life, and could someone who prays constantly be missing it?

### Opening Questions

1. Who is someone you've watched spend themselves on other people? Up close, did it look like a sacrifice, or like something else?

2. Think about your own work or daily responsibilities. Is there a difference, in how it actually feels, between doing them just to get them done and doing them for the people they serve?

3. When in your life have you felt most connected to other people? What were you doing together?

### Reading

If service is how God's light reaches the world, what does it actually look like? Baha'is look to Abdu'l-Baha, whose very name means "the Servant of Baha," and the striking thing is how ordinary his service was. He visited the sick; by one account an elderly couple received twenty visits from him in a single month. He loved the poor. In New York he emptied his pockets among them, saying *"I am in love with the poor,"* and he taught that *"no deed of man is greater before God than helping the poor."* When an early believer, Lua Getsinger, came back disgusted from a sick man's filthy room, he sent her straight back: *"Serve thy fellow man, for in him thou dost see the image and likeness of God."* There it is again, the image of God, this time in the person right in front of us.

Much of this happens through the most ordinary channel there is: our work. The Writings don't treat a profession as something separate from a life of service. Abdu'l-Baha taught that *"if a man engageth with all his power in the acquisition of a science or in the perfection of an art, it is as if he hath been worshipping God."* The test isn't what the job is; it's how it's done, with real care, and for the sake of the people it serves rather than only to get ahead.

Service also doesn't stand on its own. The Writings present prayer, meditation, study of the teachings, and service as four practices that feed one another. Prayer and meditation turn the heart toward God. Study fills it with something worth giving. Service carries it out into the world, and then sends you back to prayer and study needing more. None of them works fully without the others, and together they are the real path of spiritual growth. Take any one away and the rest begin to dry up.

There is also service through the community itself. Baha'is have no clergy. The affairs of each local community are guided by an elected council, and the Writings describe these modest bodies in surprising language, as *"shining lamps and heavenly gardens"* in which *"the spirit of life streameth in every direction."* This kind of service asks that order and efficiency always come with *"an equal degree of love, of devotion, and of spiritual development."*

One form of service the Writings lift above the rest is teaching, sharing the message of Baha'u'llah with others. It anchors the central activities of community life: the gatherings for prayer, the classes for children and young teens, the study circles where people explore the teachings together. And the same principle runs through teaching: a teacher's job is not to convince anyone, but to get out of the way:

> One should remember it is not the individual who confirms another, but the Holy Spirit which confirms. Thus the individual must become as a reed, through which the spirit may descend, and quicken souls. Thus the best way to develop capacity in teaching the Faith is to teach. As one teaches, he gains more knowledge himself, he relies more on the guidance of the spirit, and expands his own character.

Shoghi Effendi, the Faith's appointed Guardian, called this the highest service of all: *"The greatest service the Baha'is can render their countrymen, and indeed the whole of suffering mankind, is to pursue confidently and uninterruptedly the task of establishing the Faith of the Father among all men."* He named it the priority, not the only service. Relieving immediate suffering still matters. But the deepest gift is to help someone find the source of all of it for themselves.

Step back and a quiet surprise comes into view. A life poured out this way doesn't drain the person living it. It turns out to be the most fulfilling life there is, binding us to others in the strongest bonds we ever form and drawing us nearer to God in the process. And it is open to everyone, not the gifted few, but anyone willing to become a clear channel. The Universal House of Justice gathered the whole of it into a single charge:

> Ultimately, the power to transform the world is effected by love, love originating from the relationship with the divine, love ablaze among members of a community, love extended without restriction to every human being. This divine love, ignited by the Word of God, is disseminated by enkindled souls through intimate conversations that create new susceptibilities in human hearts, open minds to moral persuasion, and loosen the hold of biased norms and social systems so that they can gradually take on a new form… You are channels for this divine love; let it flow through you to all who cross your path.

Each of us is a channel. The work of a life is simply to keep it clear.

### Questions for Discussion

1. The reading calls teaching the Faith the "greatest service," above feeding the hungry or housing the homeless, though it insists both matter. Sitting with someone in real need, how would you weigh meeting that need against sharing something that could reorient their whole life? Can you hold both honestly?

2. Prayer, meditation, study, and service are described as feeding one another. Be honest about your own life: which of these do you lean on, and which do you neglect? What happens when one drops out?

3. The reading ends by calling each of us "a channel" whose only job is to keep it clear. In practice, what clogs the channel for you? And have you ever felt it actually flow?
