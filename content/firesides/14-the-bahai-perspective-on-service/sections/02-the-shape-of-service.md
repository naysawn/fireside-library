If service is how God's light reaches the world, what does it actually look like day to day? It takes many shapes.

### Orientation Toward Service

Perhaps the clearest compass for that orientation comes from Baha'u'llah:

> Be generous in prosperity, and thankful in adversity. Be worthy of the trust of thy neighbor, and look upon him with a bright and friendly face. Be a treasure to the poor, an admonisher to the rich, an answerer of the cry of the needy, a preserver of the sanctity of thy pledge. Be fair in thy judgment, and guarded in thy speech. Be unjust to no man, and show all meekness to all men. Be as a lamp unto them that walk in darkness, a joy to the sorrowful, a sea for the thirsty, a haven for the distressed, an upholder and defender of the victim of oppression. Let integrity and uprightness distinguish all thine acts. Be a home for the stranger, a balm to the suffering, a tower of strength for the fugitive. Be eyes to the blind, and a guiding light unto the feet of the erring. Be an ornament to the countenance of truth, a crown to the brow of fidelity, a pillar of the temple of righteousness, a breath of life to the body of mankind. (Bahá'u'lláh, Gleanings)

It's a high standard. The only person who entirely lived up to it was Baha'u'llah's own son, who was so dedicated to service that the title he took for himself was "the Servant of Baha." He provides us with an example of how to manifest such an attitude of service in one's own life.

Abdu'l-Baha's whole life was dedicated to serving others. From morning until night He visited the sick, gave away His possessions and money to the poor, and met with individuals and groups explaining the mysteries of God's Faith. He also advised others that they, too, should orient their lives toward service.

One story that is frequently told is the story of Lua Getsinger being asked to take care of an ill man:

One day Lua was with Abdu'l-Baha when He told her that He was too busy to call upon a friend of His who was very ill and poor, and that He wished her to go in His place — to take him food and care for him as Abdu'l-Baha had been doing. He told her where the man was to be found, and she went gladly, proud that Abdu'l-Baha should trust her with this mission. She returned quickly. "Master," she exclaimed, "surely you cannot realize to what a terrible place you sent me. I almost fainted from the awful stench, the filthy rooms, the degrading condition of that man and his house. I fled lest I contract some terrible disease."

Sadly and sternly, Abdu'l-Baha said to her, *"Dost thou desire to serve God? Serve thy fellow man, for in him dost thou see the image and likeness of God."* He told her to go back to the man's house: if it was filthy, she should clean it; if her brother was dirty, bathe him; if he was hungry, feed him. She was not to return until it was done. Many times had He done this for the man Himself — could she not serve him once?

The lesson Abdu'l-Baha wished to instill upon Lua is not sentimental sympathy but a fundamental spiritual principle: the face of God is to be found in every human being, and taking care of another is therefore sacred service.

### Our Professions

Serving the person in front of us isn't reserved for dramatic moments. Most of it happens through the most ordinary channel there is: our daily work. The Baha'i writings raise all work — arts, sciences, crafts, trades, commerce, and the professions — to the rank of worship, on one condition: that it be done with sincere effort, from the highest motives, and oriented toward the service of humanity.

When asked in London whether prayer could take the form of action, Abdu'l-Baha answered directly:

> "In the Bahá'í Cause arts, sciences and all crafts are (counted as) worship. The man who makes a piece of notepaper to the best of his ability, conscientiously, concentrating all his forces on perfecting it, is giving praise to God. Briefly, all effort and exertion put forth by man from the fullness of his heart is worship, if it is prompted by the highest motives and the will to do service to humanity. This is worship: to serve mankind and to minister to the needs of the people. Service is prayer. A physician ministering to the sick, gently, tenderly, free from prejudice and believing in the solidarity of the human race, he is giving praise."

### Teaching the Faith

If all honest work can be worship, the Writings still lift one form of service above the rest: sharing the message of Baha'u'llah with others. Shoghi Effendi called this the highest service of all: *"The greatest service the Baha'is can render their countrymen, and indeed the whole of suffering mankind, is to pursue confidently and uninterruptedly the task of establishing the Faith of the Father among all men."* 

Why hold it so high? Shoghi Effendi points to the root of the world's troubles:

> Indeed, the chief reason for the evils now rampant in society is a lack of spirituality. The materialistic civilization of our age has so much absorbed the energy and interest of mankind, that people in general no longer feel the necessity of raising themselves above the forces and conditions of their daily material existence. There is not sufficient demand for things that we should call spiritual to differentiate them from the needs and requirements of our physical existence. The universal crisis affecting mankind is, therefore, essentially spiritual in its causes. The spirit of the age, taken on the whole, is irreligious. (Shoghi Effendi)

Most of the world has still never heard the name Baha'u'llah, and only those who have themselves been moved by His teachings can carry them to others. The Universal House of Justice puts the urgency plainly:

> "There is no other aggregation of human beings who can claim to have raised up a system with the demonstrated capacity to unite all of God's children in one world-embracing Order. This achievement places us not only in a position of incomparable strength, but more particularly in one of inescapable responsibility. Does not every one of us therefore have a divine obligation to fulfill, a sacred duty to perform towards every other one who is not yet aware of the call of God's latest Manifestation? Time does not stop, does not wait. With every passing hour a fresh affliction strikes at a distracted humanity. Dare we linger?" (Universal House of Justice, 1998)

### Serving the Community

Service also flows through the life of the community itself. Baha'is have no clergy. The affairs of each community — local, national, and international — are guided by elected Baha'i councils. Of the local assemblies the Writings speak in surprising language, calling them *"shining lamps and heavenly gardens"* in which *"the spirit of life streameth in every direction."* This kind of service asks that order and efficiency always come with *"an equal degree of love, of devotion, and of spiritual development."*

Beyond these elected bodies, members serve in countless other capacities too. Some organize the observance of Holy Days, some facilitate discussion groups for newcomers, some teach classes for children and youth. And service isn't only what an institution asks of you: each person is encouraged to use their own ingenuity, finding fresh ways to meet needs and move the community forward.

### A Meaningful Life 

Underneath every one of these forms — work, teaching, community — service is about one thing: opening ourselves as instruments for divine love to flow through us. The Universal House of Justice explains:

> Ultimately, the power to transform the world is effected by love, love originating from the relationship with the divine, love ablaze among members of a community, love extended without restriction to every human being. This divine love, ignited by the Word of God, is disseminated by enkindled souls through intimate conversations that create new susceptibilities in human hearts, open minds to moral persuasion, and loosen the hold of biased norms and social systems so that they can gradually take on a new form… You are channels for this divine love; let it flow through you to all who cross your path. (Universal House of Justice, 2020)

There's a surprise waiting in all of this. A life poured out in service doesn't drain the person living it — it turns out to be the most fulfilling life there is, binding us to others and drawing us nearer to God in the process. And it asks nothing extraordinary to begin — only the willingness to become a clear channel, and to start with whoever is in front of you. That is how God's light reaches the world: one ordinary act of service at a time.
