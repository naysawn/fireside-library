# Prayer

## Section 1: Why We Pray and What It Does

### Questions for Reflection

1. A connection with God is stressed in every major faith tradition. What has been your experience with connecting with the divine? Have you ever used prayer in this way?

2. Some people pray regularly; others have never tried it or gave it up long ago. Where do you fall on that spectrum, and what shaped that?

3. When life feels overwhelming or deeply joyful, do you feel an impulse to reach out to something beyond yourself? What does that impulse feel like?

### Reading

Why do we pray? If God already knows what is in our hearts, what is the point of putting it into words?

Abdu'l-Baha, the son of Baha'u'llah (the founder of the Baha'i Faith), answers this with a simple analogy. When one friend loves another, is it not natural to want to say so? Even if the other person already knows, the impulse to express it is real. He explains: *"It is true that God knows the wishes of all hearts; but the impulse to pray is a natural one, springing from man's love to God."* [1] Prayer, in other words, is not information for God. It is an expression of a relationship. Like a child calling out for a parent who is already in the room, the act of reaching out is itself the point.

And what happens when we do reach out? Abdu'l-Baha describes prayer as the sweetest experience available to us:

> "There is nothing sweeter in the world of existence than prayer. … It creates spirituality, creates mindfulness and celestial feelings, begets new attractions of the Kingdom and engenders the susceptibilities of the higher intelligence." [2]

For those uncertain about how to begin, he offers reassurance: *"Prayer need not be in words, but rather in thought and attitude."* [3] Prayer can start with something as quiet as turning the heart in the direction of God.

The Universal House of Justice, the international governing body of the Baha'i Faith, paints a vivid picture of what this turning does to the soul:

> "Prayer is the essential spiritual conversation of the soul with its Maker, direct and without intermediation. Like the morning's dew, it brings freshness to the heart and cleanses it, purifying it from attachments of the insistent self. It is a fire that burns away the veils and a light that leads to the ocean of reunion with the Almighty." [4]

The image is worth sitting with. Dew does not force its way into the ground; it settles gently and brings life. Prayer works the same way, not through effort or strain, but through a quiet turning of the heart.

Abdu'l-Baha takes this further with another image:

> "When one supplicates to his Lord, turns to Him and seeks bounty from His Ocean, this supplication brings light to his heart, illumination to his sight, life to his soul and exaltation to his being. When the vessel is enlarged the water increases, and when the thirst grows the bounty of the cloud becomes agreeable to the taste of man. This is the mystery of supplication and the wisdom of stating one's wants." [5]

This is the key. Prayer does not merely ask for more. It enlarges the vessel itself. A person who prays regularly is not simply asking God for things; that person is being gradually reshaped, the way a riverbed is slowly widened by the water that runs through it. Our capacity to receive, to understand, and to serve grows through the practice of sincere prayer.

### Questions for Discussion

1. Abdu'l-Baha says the impulse to pray springs naturally from love. Does that match your experience? Have you ever felt pulled toward prayer (or something like it) without anyone telling you to?

2. The reading describes prayer as something that enlarges our inner vessel, increasing our capacity rather than simply giving us more. Have you experienced a time when a spiritual practice seemed to change you rather than simply change your circumstances?

3. Abdu'l-Baha says prayer "need not be in words." If prayer can be as simple as a thought or an attitude, what does that open up for someone who has never prayed before, or who left it behind?

## Section 2: The Gift of Revealed Prayer

### Questions for Reflection

1. Think of a time you did something kind or generous with absolutely no expectation of anything in return. What motivated you? How did it feel different from when you expected recognition?

2. Have you ever read or heard words that seemed to reach deeper than your own thoughts could go, that expressed something you felt but could not articulate yourself? What was that experience like?

3. Do you think it is possible to separate your inner life (your thoughts, prayers, intentions) from the way you act in the world? Or are they always connected?

### Reading

If prayer is a conversation with God, what spirit should we bring to it? The Bab, the forerunner of Baha'u'llah and co-founder of the Baha'i Faith, offers a striking answer: true worship remains the same whether its outcome is paradise or punishment.

> "Worship thou God in such wise that if thy worship lead thee to the fire, no alteration in thine adoration would be produced, and so likewise if thy recompense should be paradise. Thus and thus alone should be the worship which befitteth the one True God. Shouldst thou worship Him because of fear, this would be unseemly in the sanctified Court of His presence, and could not be regarded as an act by thee dedicated to the Oneness of His Being." [6]

This is a challenging standard. Most of us, if we are honest, have turned to God mainly in moments of crisis or need. The Bab is not condemning that impulse, but He is pointing toward something deeper: a relationship with God built on who God is, not on what we hope to get. Like a plant turning toward sunlight simply because that is its nature, the soul that has recognized God finds itself drawn naturally into conversation with Him.

But how do we sustain that conversation? The Baha'i Faith offers something distinctive here. Baha'u'llah, Abdu'l-Baha, and the Bab did not merely teach about prayer. They revealed prayers, specific words given to humanity to use. These are not ordinary compositions. Shoghi Effendi, the Guardian of the Baha'i Faith, explains that certain prayers *"have been invested by Baha'u'llah with a special potency and significance, and should therefore be accepted as such and be recited by the believers with unquestioning faith and confidence, that through them they may enter into a much closer communion with God."* [7]

Consider what this means. When we struggle to find the right words, when our own vocabulary feels small next to what we are feeling, the revealed prayers offer language that reaches further than our own minds can go. Baha'u'llah Himself models this in one of His prayers:

> "I beseech Thee … to make of my prayer a fire that will burn away the veils which have shut me out from Thy beauty, and a light that will lead me unto the ocean of Thy Presence." [8]

And He instructs us to use these words with care and attention: *"Intone, O My servant, the verses of God that have been received by thee, as intoned by them who have drawn nigh unto Him, that the sweetness of thy melody may kindle thine own soul, and attract the hearts of all men."* [9] The way we say the words matters. Reciting a prayer with attention and reverence is itself a spiritual act that transforms both the one who prays and those who hear.

Prayer does not stop at the inner life, though. Abdu'l-Baha explains that prayer and action are partners, not alternatives: *"Prayer is like the spirit and material means are like the human hand. The spirit operateth through the instrumentality of the hand."* [10] And Abdu'l-Baha illustrates God's responsiveness with a striking image from nature: *"Spirit has influence; prayer has spiritual effect. … The plant prays potentially, 'O God! Send me rain!' God answers the prayer, and the plant grows. God will answer anyone."* [11] Just as rain answers the silent prayer of a seed, God responds to every sincere turning of the heart. But Shoghi Effendi reminds us that with prayer *"must go also action and example, as these are the tangible results of the former. Both are essential."* [12] The spirit animates the hand. Prayer informs and empowers effort. One without the other is incomplete.

### Questions for Discussion

1. The Bab teaches that true worship should be independent of reward or punishment. What would it actually look like to pray "out of love alone"? Is that realistic, or is it an ideal we can only move toward?

2. The reading describes revealed prayers as having a "special potency." Have you ever experienced words, whether in prayer, poetry, or scripture, that seemed to do something to you beyond what you could explain? What made those words different from ordinary language?

3. Abdu'l-Baha compares prayer to the spirit and action to the hand. How do you understand the relationship between your inner life and your outward engagement with the world? Can one thrive without the other?
