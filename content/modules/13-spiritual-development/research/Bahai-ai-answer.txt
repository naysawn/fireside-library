DIRECT ANSWER
In the Bahá'í writings, polishing mirrors is a central metaphor for spiritual development and the refinement of the human heart to receive divine light. It describes the process of removing spiritual obstacles—the "dust and dross" of material attachment and ego—so that one can increasingly reflect the attributes and knowledge of God, just as a polished mirror perfectly reflects the sun's light.

EVIDENCE
The Human Heart as a Mirror for Divine Light
The heart in Bahá'í teaching is understood fundamentally as a mirror whose purpose is to reflect divine reality. 'Abdu'l-Bahá explains: "The most important thing is to polish the mirrors of hearts in order that they may become illumined and receptive of the divine light. One heart may possess the capacity of the polished mirror; another, be covered and obscured by the dust and dross of this world." 
1
PU The principle is grounded in a simple law of nature: "The light of the sun becomes apparent in each object according to the capacity of that object. The difference is simply one of degree and receptivity." 
2
PU A heart that has been polished achieves clarity and capacity, while one obscured remains unable to reflect divine bounty, even though God's generosity continues to shine equally upon all.

Polishing as Spiritual Responsibility and Practice
The polishing of mirrors is framed as a fundamental human duty. 'Abdu'l-Bahá states explicitly: "Therefore, our duty lies in seeking to polish the mirrors of our hearts in order that we shall become reflectors of that light and recipients of the divine bounties which may be fully revealed through them." 
3
BW This is not a passive state but an active spiritual practice—a sustained effort to remove impediments. In a letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, the polishing process is connected to spiritual testing and adversity: "Suffering, of one kind or another, seems to be the portion of man in this world... they seem to be part of the polish God employs to make us finer, and enable us to reflect more of His attributes!" 
4
LG Thus suffering itself becomes understood as a divinely employed instrument for the refinement and clarification of the soul.

The Distinction Between Mirror and Light
The Bahá'í teachings emphasize a crucial spiritual principle: one must distinguish between the divine light itself (God) and the mirrors that reflect it (human souls or the Manifestations of God). 'Abdu'l-Bahá warns: "There are certain souls who are lovers of the Sun; they perceive the effulgence of the Sun from every mirror. They are not fettered or attached to the mirrors; they are attached to the Sun itself and adore it, no matter from what point it may shine. But those who adore the mirror and are attached to it become deprived of witnessing the light of the Sun when it shines forth from another mirror." 
5
PU The Báb similarly teaches: "He—glorified be His mention—resembleth the sun. Were unnumbered mirrors to be placed before it, each would, according to its capacity, reflect the splendor of that sun, and were none to be placed before it, it would still continue to rise and set, and the mirrors alone would be veiled from its light." 
6
SW This distinction safeguards believers from idolatry and ensures spiritual focus remains on the divine source.

Purity, Clarity, and Spiritual Transformation
The condition of purity and clarity is essential to the mirror's function. A source states: "Souls are like unto mirrors, and the bounty of God is like unto the sun. When the mirrors pass beyond all coloring and attain purity and polish, and are confronted with the sun, they will reflect in full perfection its light and glory." 
7
BW The language of "passing beyond all coloring" suggests the removal of personal prejudice, self-interest, and attachment to worldly values. In a modern application, the Universal House of Justice addresses youth: "strengthen your practice of those spiritual disciplines enjoined by Bahá'u'lláh that help you polish the mirrors of your hearts to reflect the attributes of God." 
8
UHJ20221101 This reflects the continuity of the metaphor from early teachings to contemporary guidance.

Relationship to Knowledge and Arts
The metaphor extends into the domain of knowledge and creative expression. Bahá'u'lláh teaches: "The Sun of Truth is the Word of God... Its appearance in every mirror is conditioned by the colour of that mirror. For instance, when its light is cast upon the mirrors of the hearts of the wise, it bringeth forth wisdom. In like manner when it manifesteth itself in the mirrors of the hearts of craftsmen, it unfoldeth new and unique arts." 
9
IA Here, polishing one's mirror determines not only spiritual receptivity but also the quality and originality of intellectual and artistic work one can produce.

NUANCE
The sources are consistent in presenting polishing mirrors as a metaphorical and spiritual practice rather than a literal act. The "dust and dross" obscuring the mirror refers to materialistic attachment, ego, prejudice, and spiritual negligence—not physical or external debris. Additionally, while the metaphor emphasizes human effort and responsibility ("our duty"), the sources also make clear that the ultimate source of transformation is divine grace and bounty, not human will alone. The mirror is refined so that it can receive the divine light, but the light itself remains eternally independent of the mirror's condition; as 'Abdu'l-Bahá notes, "so far as the Sun itself is concerned it is shining thereon and is neither lessened nor deprived." 
10
PU Finally, the imagery of diverse mirrors reflecting the same light is used by multiple authors (Bahá'u'lláh, 'Abdu'l-Bahá, and the Báb) to support the principle of unity amid diversity—each person, like each mirror, reflects divine qualities according to individual capacity, yet all reflect the one eternal source.

DIRECT ANSWER
The Bahá'í writings address daily spiritual growth through concrete practices — especially prayer, meditation, and self-examination each morning — while identifying internal barriers like the "dust and dross of this world" that cloud the heart, and external barriers like self-interest, prejudice, and condescension that block community life. Being deliberate means actively polishing the mirror of the heart, acting with love, and comparing today's spiritual state to yesterday's.

EVIDENCE
Daily Self-Examination as the Core Practice
The most concrete daily instruction comes from 'Abdu'l-Bahá: "Every day, in the morning when arising one should compare today with yesterday and see in what condition you are. If you see your belief is stronger and your heart more occupied with God and your love increased and your freedom from the world greater then thank God and ask for the increase of these qualities. You must begin to pray and repent for all that you have done which is wrong and you must implore and ask for help and assistance that you may become better than yesterday so that you may continue to make progress."
1
IA This is a remarkably practical framework — not abstract aspiration but a morning check-in with specific criteria: strength of belief, occupation of heart with God, growth of love, and freedom from worldly attachment.

Prayer and meditation are named as indispensable partners to this self-examination. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi states: "Prayer and meditation are very important factors in deepening the spiritual life of the individual, but with them must go also action and example, as these are the tangible results of the former. Both are essential." 
2
IA Meditation is also described as a door to deeper knowledge: "Through meditation the doors of deeper knowledge and inspiration may be opened." 
3
IA The manner of meditation is left to the individual, but the consistent teaching is that inner work must manifest outwardly in deeds.

Polishing the Mirror of the Heart
'Abdu'l-Bahá offers a powerful image for what daily spiritual practice is actually doing. He compares the human heart to a mirror: "The most important thing is to polish the mirrors of hearts in order that they may become illumined and receptive of the divine light. One heart may possess the capacity of the polished mirror; another, be covered and obscured by the dust and dross of this world. Although the same Sun is shining upon both, in the mirror which is polished, pure and sanctified you may behold the Sun in all its fullness, glory and power, revealing its majesty and effulgence; but in the mirror which is rusted and obscured there is no capacity for reflection." 
4
PU The practical implication is direct: daily effort is required precisely because the world continuously deposits "dust and dross" on the heart. Neglect accumulates; deliberate practice reverses it.

The Universal House of Justice echoes this same language when addressing youth, urging them to "strengthen your practice of those spiritual disciplines enjoined by Bahá'u'lláh that help you polish the mirrors of your hearts to reflect the attributes of God." 
5
UHJ20221101 They point to 'Abdu'l-Bahá's own life as the model — "how He lived His life as a daily sacrifice to the cause of the oneness of humanity, how He embraced with selfless love all people who crossed His path, how He saw reflected in each soul the image of a loving God." 
6
UHJ20221101 Being deliberate, in this framing, means actively seeing the image of God in every person encountered throughout the day.

The Inner Barrier: Worldly Self-Engagement
'Abdu'l-Bahá directly names what pulls against daily spiritual practice: "Today the nations of the world are self-engaged, occupied with mortal and transitory accomplishments, consumed by the fires of passion and self. Self is dominant; enmity and animosity prevail." 
7
PU The antidote he prescribes is equally direct: "you must deal with all in loving-kindness," "order your lives in accordance with the first principle of the divine teaching, which is love," and "strive to attain a station of absolute love one toward another. By the absence of love, enmity increases. By the exercise of love, love strengthens and enmities dwindle away." 
8
PU The practical instruction is that love is not only a feeling to cultivate internally but a posture to exercise actively — and the exercise itself builds the capacity.

Barriers in Community Life: Consultation and Participation
At the community level, the Universal House of Justice identifies concrete behavioral barriers that must be named and addressed rather than tolerated. These include "the consultation moving at a speed which confuses him, language being used which he cannot understand, behavioral characteristics which unwittingly express condescension leading to the humiliation of others, or a feeling that one is being ignored." 
9
UHJ19890622 The prescribed response is not passive endurance but frank speech: "If an Assembly member feels that there are barriers affecting the consultation of the body, he should frankly and courageously raise his concerns." 
10
UHJ19890622

This frankness requires individual courage alongside humility. The Guardian writes that "it is not only the right but the sacred obligation of every member to express freely and openly his views, without being afraid of displeasing or alienating any of his fellow members." 
11
UHJ19890622 At the same time, no one should feel disqualified by inexperience: "Not infrequently, nay oftentimes, the most lowly, untutored and inexperienced among the friends will, by the sheer inspiring force of selfless and ardent devotion, contribute a distinct and memorable share to a highly involved discussion."
12
UHJ19890622 Being deliberate in community life means showing up fully, speaking honestly, and trusting that sincere devotion counts for more than credentials.

Barriers of Prejudice and the Need for Sustained Resistance
Shoghi Effendi is frank that even within the Bahá'í community, racial prejudice remains a real and admitted barrier: "He is well aware that the conditions within the ranks of the believers in respect to race prejudice is far from being as it should be." 
13
UL His practical counsel is to resist letting awareness of others' shortcomings become an obstacle to one's own service — "They must accept the Cause of Bahá'u'lláh for the sake of the Cause, love it, and cling to it, and teach it, and fight for it as their own Cause, forgetful of the shortcomings of others." 
14
UL The House of Justice extends this to the present, urging believers to "foster among yourselves enduring spiritual bonds that resist the forces of prejudice" and to "resist every influence to give primacy to ethnicity." 
15
UHJ20221101 The word "resist" is significant — these forces do not neutralize themselves; they require active, daily opposition.

NUANCE
The sources make clear that daily spiritual practice involves both a personal, inward dimension (prayer, meditation, morning self-examination, polishing the heart) and an outward, communal dimension (honest participation, loving action, resisting prejudice). Neither alone is sufficient — Shoghi Effendi explicitly states that prayer and meditation "must go also" with "action and example, as these are the tangible results of the former." 
16
IA The writings do not offer a single prescribed method for meditation beyond the obligatory prayers, leaving considerable room for personal latitude, but the criteria for measuring progress are specific: Is belief stronger? Is the heart more occupied with God? Is love growing? Is freedom from worldly attachment increasing? These are the daily metrics 'Abdu'l-Bahá actually names.
